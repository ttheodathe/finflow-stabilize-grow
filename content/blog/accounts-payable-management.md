# Accounts Payable Management for Small Business

Accounts payable (AP) is the money a business owes to its suppliers and vendors for goods and services received but not yet paid for. Managing it well keeps suppliers happy, avoids late fees and duplicate payments, and gives you an accurate picture of what the business owes.

## What Is Accounts Payable?

When a supplier sends a bill and you agree to pay it later, the amount you owe is recorded as a liability called accounts payable. It appears on the balance sheet as a current liability when payment is due within a year. When the bill is paid, the liability is reduced.

Accounts payable is different from other spending:

- **Expenses paid immediately** (such as a card purchase) never become payable.
- **Payroll and taxes** are usually tracked separately from supplier bills.
- **Accounts receivable** is the opposite side: money customers owe you. See [Accounts Receivable Management](/blog/accounts-receivable-management).

## The Accounts Payable Process

A reliable AP process follows the same steps every time:

1. **Receive the bill.** Collect supplier invoices in one place rather than across emails and paper.
2. **Check it.** Confirm the supplier, the amount, the tax and the terms. Compare it with the purchase order or the delivery or work received.
3. **Record it.** Enter the bill with the supplier, date, due date, amount and the correct expense or asset category.
4. **Approve it.** Someone with authority confirms that it should be paid.
5. **Schedule it.** Plan payment for the due date, or earlier if an early-payment discount makes sense.
6. **Pay it.** Pay by the agreed method and record the payment against the bill.
7. **Reconcile.** Match payments with the bank statement and keep the supporting documents.

## Match Before You Pay

Where possible, compare three things before paying: the purchase order (what you asked for), the delivery or completion record (what you received), and the supplier invoice (what you are being charged). This is often called three-way matching. Even a small business benefits from a lighter version: confirm that you actually received what the invoice says and that the price agrees with the quote.

## Set Up Basic Controls

Controls protect the business from mistakes and fraud, even in a small team.

- **Separate duties where possible.** The person who approves a payment should not be the only one who records and releases it.
- **Verify new suppliers and changed bank details.** Confirm bank detail changes through a trusted contact method, not just an email request, because invoice fraud often involves altered payment details.
- **Watch for duplicates.** Check supplier name, invoice number and amount before paying so the same bill is not paid twice.
- **Keep an approval record.** Note who approved each payment and when.
- **Limit access.** Only people who need to release payments should be able to do so.

## Timing Payments

When you pay affects your cash flow. Paying every bill immediately reduces your cash sooner than necessary, while paying late can bring penalties and damage relationships.

- Pay on or just before the due date unless a discount changes the math.
- Consider early-payment discounts carefully. For example, terms written as "2/10, net 30" usually mean a 2 percent discount if paid within 10 days, otherwise the full amount is due in 30 days. Whether that is worth taking depends on your cash position and borrowing costs.
- Group payments into regular payment runs, such as weekly, so the process stays predictable.
- Keep tax and payroll obligations separate and visible, since these have less flexibility.

See [Small Business Cash Flow Management](/blog/small-business-cash-flow-management) for how payment timing fits into your wider cash picture.

## Manage Supplier Relationships

Suppliers are more likely to be flexible when you are reliable and communicate early.

- Pay on time as a default.
- If you expect a delay, contact the supplier before the due date and agree on a new date.
- Ask for terms that suit your cash cycle, and review them periodically.
- Keep contact details, payment terms and bank details up to date in one place.

## Use an AP Aging Report

An accounts payable aging report lists unpaid bills grouped by how long they have been outstanding, such as current, 1 to 30 days overdue, 31 to 60 days and so on. It helps you:

- Spot bills that are close to or past their due date.
- Plan the next payment run.
- See how much you owe each supplier.
- Check that the payable balance on your balance sheet matches the detailed bills.

## Common Accounts Payable Mistakes

- Paying a bill twice because it arrived by email and by post.
- Missing early-payment discounts or late-fee deadlines.
- Recording a bill in the wrong period, which distorts your expenses.
- Paying without checking that goods or services were received.
- Letting bills pile up unrecorded until month end.
- Having no record of who approved a payment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is accounts payable an expense?

No. Accounts payable is a liability: the amount you owe. The related cost is recorded as an expense or asset when the bill is recorded, and paying the bill reduces the liability.

### What is the difference between accounts payable and accounts receivable?

Accounts payable is money you owe to suppliers. Accounts receivable is money customers owe you.

### How often should I pay bills?

Many businesses use a regular payment run, such as weekly, and pay on or shortly before due dates. The right rhythm depends on your cash flow and supplier terms.

### Should I always take early-payment discounts?

Not always. Compare the discount with your cash position and any cost of borrowing. If paying early would leave you short of cash, the discount may not be worth it.
