# Chart of Accounts for Small Business: A Complete Guide With Examples

A **chart of accounts (COA)** is the organized list of accounts a business uses to record and classify its financial transactions.

It is one of the foundations of good bookkeeping. Every invoice, payment, expense, asset purchase, loan, owner contribution, and other financial transaction ultimately needs to be assigned to an appropriate account.

A well-designed chart of accounts helps a small business answer practical questions such as:

- How much revenue did we generate?
- What are our biggest expenses?
- How much cash and other assets do we have?
- How much do we owe?
- How much is invested in the business?
- Which costs are increasing?
- What should appear on the profit and loss statement?
- What should appear on the balance sheet?

A poorly designed chart can create the opposite problem: too many accounts, inconsistent classifications, duplicate categories, and financial reports that are difficult to understand.

This guide explains how a small business can create a practical chart of accounts and keep it useful as the business grows.

## What Is a Chart of Accounts?

A chart of accounts is a structured list of the accounts used by a business to categorize financial transactions.

Each account normally has:

- An account name
- An account number or code
- An account type
- A purpose or description

For example, a small service business might use accounts such as:

| Account Number | Account Name | Account Type |
|---|---|---|
| 1000 | Cash | Asset |
| 1100 | Accounts Receivable | Asset |
| 2000 | Accounts Payable | Liability |
| 3000 | Owner's Equity | Equity |
| 4000 | Service Revenue | Revenue |
| 5000 | Advertising Expense | Expense |
| 5100 | Software Expense | Expense |

The exact numbering system is flexible. What matters most is consistency.

A chart of accounts is **not the same thing as a list of every transaction**. Instead, it provides the categories into which transactions are recorded.

## Why Does a Small Business Need a Chart of Accounts?

Even a very small business benefits from a clear account structure.

Without one, transactions can gradually become inconsistent. For example, one advertising payment might be recorded as "Marketing," another as "Promotion," and another as "Advertising." The business then has three categories for essentially the same type of cost.

A good chart of accounts provides a consistent framework for bookkeeping and reporting.

### 1. It keeps transactions organized

Every transaction can be assigned to an appropriate account rather than being recorded in an ad hoc category.

### 2. It improves financial reporting

Accounting software can use the account structure to produce reports such as:

- Profit and loss statements
- Balance sheets
- Cash-flow reports
- Expense reports
- Accounts receivable reports

### 3. It makes expenses easier to analyze

Instead of seeing one large "expenses" figure, a business can distinguish rent, payroll, software, advertising, transportation, professional fees, and other meaningful categories.

### 4. It supports consistent bookkeeping

A defined account structure makes it easier for the owner, bookkeeper, accountant, or finance assistant to classify transactions consistently.

### 5. It can grow with the business

A simple chart can start with the accounts a business actually needs and become more detailed when reporting requirements become more complex.

## The Five Main Types of Accounts

Most small-business charts of accounts are organized around five fundamental account categories:

1. Assets
2. Liabilities
3. Equity
4. Revenue
5. Expenses

These categories correspond to the basic accounting structure used in financial reporting.

### 1. Assets

Assets are resources controlled by the business that have economic value.

Common small-business asset accounts include:

- Cash
- Bank accounts
- Accounts receivable
- Inventory
- Prepaid expenses
- Equipment
- Vehicles
- Buildings
- Accumulated depreciation

Assets are generally divided into current and non-current assets.

**Current assets** are expected to be converted into cash, sold, or used within the normal operating cycle or generally within a year.

**Non-current assets** are resources expected to provide value over a longer period.

### 2. Liabilities

Liabilities are amounts the business owes to other parties.

Examples include:

- Accounts payable
- Credit card balances
- Short-term loans
- Long-term loans
- Payroll liabilities
- Tax liabilities
- Accrued expenses

Liabilities may also be classified as current or non-current depending on when they are expected to be settled.

### 3. Equity

Equity represents the owner's or owners' residual interest in the business after liabilities are deducted from assets.

Depending on the business structure, equity accounts may include:

- Owner's capital
- Owner contributions
- Owner draws
- Retained earnings
- Common stock
- Additional paid-in capital

The exact equity accounts required depend on the legal and accounting structure of the business.

### 4. Revenue

Revenue accounts record income generated from the business's normal activities.

Examples include:

- Product sales
- Service revenue
- Consulting revenue
- Subscription revenue
- Commission income

A business may use one revenue account or several revenue accounts when separate revenue streams are important for management reporting.

### 5. Expenses

Expense accounts record costs incurred to operate the business.

Common examples include:

- Advertising
- Bank fees
- Insurance
- Office supplies
- Rent
- Salaries and wages
- Software subscriptions
- Telephone and internet
- Travel
- Professional fees
- Utilities
- Repairs and maintenance

The goal is not to create an account for every individual purchase. The goal is to create categories that produce useful financial information.

## How Should Account Numbers Be Designed?

Many businesses assign numerical ranges to account categories.

A common structure is:

| Number Range | Category |
|---|---|
| 1000–1999 | Assets |
| 2000–2999 | Liabilities |
| 3000–3999 | Equity |
| 4000–4999 | Revenue |
| 5000–5999 | Expenses |

The exact ranges are not mandatory.

The advantage of numerical grouping is that related accounts can be easier to locate and organize.

For example:

- 1000–1099 could represent cash and bank accounts
- 1100–1199 could represent receivables
- 1500–1599 could represent fixed assets
- 5000–5099 could represent marketing expenses
- 5500–5599 could represent technology and software expenses

Leave space between accounts when practical. A numbering system that allows future additions is easier to maintain than one that uses every number immediately.

## How Many Accounts Should a Small Business Have?

There is no single correct number.

A freelancer with a simple operation may need only a relatively small number of accounts. A retail company with inventory, multiple locations, employees, loans, and several sales channels may need substantially more.

The best rule is:

> **Use enough accounts to produce useful financial information, but not so many that bookkeeping becomes unnecessarily complicated.**

For example, a small consulting business probably does not need separate expense accounts for every type of office stationery. But it may be useful to distinguish software, professional fees, advertising, and travel if those costs are significant.

### Signs that you may have too many accounts

- Several accounts are rarely used.
- Two or more accounts mean almost the same thing.
- Staff frequently disagree about where transactions belong.
- Financial reports contain dozens of insignificant categories.
- You created accounts mainly because a single unusual transaction occurred.

### Signs that you may need more accounts

- Important revenue streams cannot be analyzed separately.
- Significant expenses are hidden inside a broad category.
- Management cannot answer an important financial question from the reports.
- Different transactions with materially different purposes are consistently grouped together.

## Parent Accounts and Subaccounts

A chart of accounts can use parent accounts and subaccounts to provide additional detail.

For example:

**5000 Marketing Expense**
- 5010 Advertising
- 5020 Social Media
- 5030 Events
- 5040 Promotional Materials

This structure allows the business to see total marketing costs while also analyzing individual categories.

Subaccounts are useful when the additional detail supports an actual business decision.

They should not be created simply because accounting software makes it possible.

## Chart of Accounts vs. General Ledger

These terms are related but not interchangeable.

The **chart of accounts** is the organized list of accounts available for recording transactions.

The **general ledger** contains the accounting activity recorded in those accounts.

For example, "Advertising Expense" can be an account in the chart of accounts. The general ledger then contains the individual transactions posted to that account.

In simple terms:

**Chart of accounts = the categories**

**General ledger = the transactions organized within those categories**

## How the Chart of Accounts Connects to Financial Statements

The chart of accounts provides the structure used to organize information for financial reporting.

### Profit and loss statement

Revenue and expense accounts generally feed into the profit and loss statement.

For example:

**Revenue**
- Service Revenue: $20,000

**Expenses**
- Advertising: $2,000
- Software: $1,000
- Rent: $3,000
- Professional Fees: $1,000

**Net profit:** $13,000

For more information about this financial statement, see [Profit and Loss Statement for Small Business](https://www.finflowtrack.com/blog/profit-and-loss-statement-small-business).

### Balance sheet

Asset, liability, and equity accounts are used in the balance sheet.

For example:

**Assets**
- Cash: $15,000
- Accounts Receivable: $5,000
- Equipment: $10,000

**Liabilities**
- Accounts Payable: $4,000
- Loan: $6,000

**Equity**
- Owner's Equity: $20,000

The balance sheet follows the fundamental relationship:

**Assets = Liabilities + Equity**

See [Balance Sheet for Small Business](https://www.finflowtrack.com/blog/balance-sheet-for-small-business) for a deeper explanation.

## The Chart of Accounts and Double-Entry Bookkeeping

In double-entry bookkeeping, each transaction affects at least two accounts.

For example, if a business pays $500 for advertising from its bank account:

- Debit: Advertising Expense $500
- Credit: Bank Account $500

The chart of accounts provides the accounts that can be used for these entries.

This is one reason account classification matters. If transactions are consistently posted to the wrong accounts, financial statements can become misleading even when the arithmetic is correct.

## Common Chart of Accounts Mistakes

### 1. Creating too many accounts

More accounts do not automatically mean better accounting.

Excessive detail can make bookkeeping slower and reports harder to interpret.

### 2. Creating duplicate accounts

For example:

- Internet
- Internet Expense
- Internet Costs

If all three represent the same type of cost, the structure is unnecessarily fragmented.

### 3. Mixing account types

An asset should not be created as an expense simply because money was spent.

For example, buying equipment that qualifies as a business asset may require different accounting treatment from buying office supplies.

### 4. Using vague account names

Names such as "Other," "Miscellaneous," or "General" should be used carefully.

If an account receives a large share of transactions, that may indicate the chart needs better categories.

### 5. Designing the chart around individual transactions

A chart of accounts should describe meaningful financial categories, not become a list of every purchase.

### 6. Changing account structures without considering reporting history

Renaming, merging, or moving accounts can affect comparisons between periods and the interpretation of historical reports.

Before making major changes, consider how the change will affect prior-period reporting and tax or audit records.

## When Should You Add a New Account?

Consider adding a new account when:

- The transaction type is materially different from existing categories.
- Management needs to monitor it separately.
- A reporting or compliance requirement requires the distinction.
- The amount is significant enough to justify separate visibility.
- Existing categories make financial analysis difficult.

For example, a business that begins spending heavily on paid advertising may benefit from separating advertising from a broad "Marketing" category.

But if a business buys a one-time $20 office item, creating a permanent new account is usually unnecessary.

## When Should You Merge or Deactivate Accounts?

You may consider merging or deactivating accounts when:

- Two accounts serve essentially the same purpose.
- One account is no longer relevant.
- Transactions have been consistently misclassified because the categories are confusing.
- The additional detail no longer provides useful information.

Before changing accounts, review the effect on historical transactions and financial reports.

In many accounting systems, it is safer to make an old account inactive rather than deleting it, especially when it has historical transactions.

## Multi-Currency Considerations

Businesses that invoice or pay suppliers in multiple currencies need to consider how currencies interact with their accounting structure.

For example, a business may have:

- USD bank account
- EUR bank account
- RWF bank account
- Foreign-currency receivables
- Foreign-currency payables

A multi-currency accounting system should distinguish between the **account itself** and the **currency in which transactions are recorded**.

Foreign-currency transactions can also create exchange-rate differences. These differences may need appropriate accounting treatment under the business's applicable accounting framework.

The chart of accounts should therefore be designed together with the accounting software's currency, exchange-rate, and reporting capabilities.

## A Practical Chart of Accounts Setup Checklist

Before finalizing your chart of accounts, check the following:

- [ ] Every bank and cash account is represented appropriately.
- [ ] Accounts receivable is included if customers buy on credit.
- [ ] Accounts payable is included if the business owes suppliers.
- [ ] Major revenue streams are identifiable.
- [ ] Significant expense categories are identifiable.
- [ ] Assets are separated from ordinary expenses where appropriate.
- [ ] Liabilities are classified appropriately.
- [ ] Equity accounts reflect the business structure.
- [ ] Account numbers follow a consistent pattern.
- [ ] Duplicate or overlapping accounts have been avoided.
- [ ] Parent and subaccounts are used only where useful.
- [ ] The structure supports the financial reports the business needs.
- [ ] The structure can accommodate reasonable future growth.
- [ ] Tax, legal, donor, or industry-specific requirements have been considered.

## How to Keep Your Chart of Accounts Clean

Creating the chart is only the first step. It should also be maintained.

### Review it periodically

Review account usage at least periodically and identify categories that are unused, duplicated, or no longer useful.

### Give accounts clear names

Names should be understandable to anyone responsible for bookkeeping.

### Document unusual classifications

If a business has an account that could easily be confused with another, a short description or bookkeeping policy can reduce errors.

### Avoid unnecessary changes

Frequent restructuring makes period-to-period comparisons more difficult.

### Use additional tracking tools when appropriate

If you need profitability by customer, project, department, location, or program, creating dozens of general-ledger accounts may not be the best solution. Where available, use dimensions, projects, classes, tags, or other tracking mechanisms.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a chart of accounts in simple terms?

A chart of accounts is a structured list of the categories a business uses to organize its financial transactions. These categories normally include assets, liabilities, equity, revenue, and expenses.

### What are the five main types of accounts?

The five main account categories are assets, liabilities, equity, revenue, and expenses.

### Does every small business need the same chart of accounts?

No. The appropriate structure depends on the business model, size, transactions, reporting needs, legal structure, and applicable accounting requirements.

### How many accounts should a small business have?

There is no universal number. A business should have enough accounts to produce useful reports without creating unnecessary bookkeeping complexity.

### Should a small business use account numbers?

Account numbers are not always required, but they can make a chart of accounts easier to organize, especially as the number of accounts grows.

### What is the difference between a chart of accounts and a general ledger?

The chart of accounts is the list of available accounting categories. The general ledger contains the transactions posted to those accounts.

### Can I change my chart of accounts later?

Yes, but changes should be made carefully. Merging, renaming, or deactivating accounts can affect historical reporting and period comparisons.

### Should every expense have its own account?

No. Accounts should be created when separate classification provides useful information. Creating an account for every minor expense can make bookkeeping unnecessarily complicated.

### How does a chart of accounts affect financial statements?

Revenue and expense accounts contribute to the profit and loss statement, while asset, liability, and equity accounts contribute to the balance sheet. The account structure therefore directly affects how financial information is organized and reported.

## Final Takeaway

A good chart of accounts is **simple enough to maintain and detailed enough to answer important business questions**.

Start with the five fundamental categories—assets, liabilities, equity, revenue, and expenses. Then add accounts based on the actual transactions and reporting needs of the business.

Avoid creating unnecessary categories simply because they are available. At the same time, do not hide significant revenue streams, assets, liabilities, or expenses inside vague accounts.

As the business grows, review the structure periodically and make changes carefully so that financial reports remain consistent and useful.
