Cash flow is the movement of money into and out of a business over a period of time. It helps answer a practical question: **does the business have enough available cash to meet the payments coming due?**

A business can report a profit and still experience a cash shortage. A customer may have received an invoice but not paid it yet, while rent, suppliers, wages, loan payments, and other bills still need to be paid.

Understanding cash flow helps business owners look beyond sales and profit figures and pay attention to the timing of receipts and payments.

## What Does Cash Flow Mean?

Cash flow describes cash received and cash paid during a particular period. Cash inflows can include customer payments, owner contributions, loan proceeds, or proceeds from selling an asset. Cash outflows can include supplier payments, operating costs, equipment purchases, loan repayments, and owner withdrawals.

The difference between total cash inflows and total cash outflows is the net cash movement for that period.

A positive net cash movement means more cash came in than went out during the period. A negative net cash movement means more cash went out than came in. Neither result, by itself, tells you whether the business is profitable.

## Cash Flow Is Not the Same as Profit

Profit and cash flow answer different questions.

- **Profit** measures income minus expenses over an accounting period, using the business's accounting method.
- **Cash flow** tracks when money is actually received and paid.
- **Available cash** is the amount the business can currently access, subject to any restrictions or pending transactions.

For example, a service business completes a $2,000 project in January and sends an invoice. The customer pays in February.

Depending on the accounting method and the applicable accounting rules, the sale may be recorded as revenue in January. But the cash does not arrive until February. If the business has supplier bills due in January, the timing difference matters.

This is one reason that a profit and loss statement should not be treated as a substitute for monitoring cash. For a related explanation, read [Profit and Loss Statement for Small Business](/blog/profit-and-loss-statement-small-business).

## The Three Main Types of Cash Flow

A cash flow statement commonly groups cash movements into three categories.

### 1. Operating activities

Operating cash flow relates to the business's normal activities. Examples can include:

- Cash collected from customers.
- Payments to suppliers.
- Rent, utilities, and other operating costs.
- Payments for services used to run the business.

Operating cash flow helps show whether day-to-day business activity is bringing in enough cash to support ongoing operations.

### 2. Investing activities

Investing cash flow relates to buying and selling long-term assets or investments. Examples include purchasing equipment, vehicles, or business property, and receiving cash from selling an asset.

A large equipment purchase may create a negative investing cash flow for the period even when the business's regular operations are generating cash.

### 3. Financing activities

Financing cash flow relates to how a business obtains and repays funding. Examples can include receiving a loan, repaying loan principal, issuing shares, or making owner distributions.

These categories help readers understand whether cash movements came from operations, investments, or financing decisions. The exact presentation depends on the applicable accounting framework.

## A Simple Cash Flow Formula

A basic cash position calculation is:

```
Beginning cash
+ Cash received
- Cash paid
= Ending cash
```

**Example only:**

| Item | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Beginning cash | $12,000 |
| Customer payments received | $8,000 |
| Payments made | ($10,500) |
| Ending cash | $9,500 |

The example shows the movement in cash during one period. It is not a forecast and does not include assumptions about future receipts or payments.

For a useful review, compare the calculated ending balance with the relevant bank and cash records. Investigate differences rather than assuming every recorded transaction has cleared.

## Common Reasons a Business Experiences Cash Flow Pressure

Cash flow pressure often comes from timing differences or large payments rather than a lack of sales alone.

### Customers pay later than expected

Outstanding invoices represent amounts customers owe. They are not the same as cash already received. Review invoice due dates, follow up on overdue balances, and understand which receipts are realistic for the period.

See [Accounts Receivable Management](/blog/accounts-receivable-management) for more on monitoring customer balances.

### Supplier and operating payments are due sooner

A business may need to pay suppliers before it receives payment from customers. Review upcoming bills and payment dates so the business can understand its short-term obligations.

See [Accounts Payable Management](/blog/accounts-payable-management) for a practical overview.

### Inventory or large purchases tie up cash

Buying inventory, equipment, or other assets can use cash before the business receives the benefit of those purchases. Consider the timing and size of planned payments alongside expected receipts.

### Sales change during the year

Seasonal demand, customer concentration, delayed projects, or unexpected interruptions can change when cash comes in. Comparing actual results with earlier expectations can help identify changes that need attention.

## How to Review Cash Flow Regularly

The right review frequency depends on transaction volume, payment timing, and how much cash uncertainty the business faces. A simple routine can include:

1. Check the current cash balance and distinguish cleared funds from pending transactions.
2. Review customer invoices, expected payment dates, and overdue balances.
3. List supplier bills and other payments due soon.
4. Identify recurring costs and any large one-off payments.
5. Compare the period's actual cash movement with the previous period.
6. Record unusual differences and follow up on their causes.

A weekly review may be useful for a business with tight cash margins or frequent transactions. A monthly review can help identify broader trends. The important point is to use a consistent process that fits the business.

The U.S. Small Business Administration recommends maintaining proper bookkeeping and reviewing key financial information as part of managing a business. ## Cash Flow Statement vs. Cash Flow Forecast

A cash flow statement is a historical report. It summarizes cash received and paid during a completed period.

A cash flow forecast is forward-looking. It estimates future receipts and payments to help a business consider what its cash position may look like later.

A statement helps answer, **what happened to cash?** A forecast helps answer, **what might happen if these expected receipts and payments occur?**

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is cash flow in simple terms?

Cash flow is the money a business receives and pays during a period. It shows how cash moves through the business, not simply how much revenue it has earned.

### Can a profitable business have negative cash flow?

Yes. A business may record revenue before customers pay, or it may make large payments during a period. Profit and cash flow use different timing, so both should be reviewed.

### What is positive cash flow?

Positive net cash flow means cash received exceeded cash paid during the period being measured. It does not automatically mean the business made a profit or that every future bill can be paid.

### What is the difference between a cash flow statement and a cash flow forecast?

A cash flow statement summarizes historical cash movements. A cash flow forecast estimates future cash receipts and payments using assumptions.

## Key Takeaway

Cash flow is about the timing of money entering and leaving a business. Monitoring it alongside profit, outstanding invoices, upcoming bills, and financial reports gives business owners a clearer view of their short-term position.

Start with accurate records, review cash movements regularly, and investigate timing gaps early. For planning ahead, use a separate forecast and update it as actual payments and receipts change.
