# Cash Book vs. Sales Book vs. Ledger: What's the Difference?

A sales book, cash book, and general ledger are related accounting records, but they do not record the same event.

A **sales book** records sales transactions. A **cash book** records cash and bank receipts and payments. A **general ledger** organizes transactions by account so the business can prepare financial reports.

The distinction matters when a business sells goods on credit, receives a deposit, or gets paid several days after an invoice is issued. If the sale and the later receipt are treated as one event, the business can overstate cash received or record the same amount twice.

## 1. What does a sales book record?

A sales book is a record of sales made by the business. Depending on the accounting system, it may focus on credit sales, while cash sales may be summarized through a cash register or cash receipts record.

Useful sales information includes:
- Date of sale
- Invoice or receipt reference
- Customer
- Products or services sold
- Quantity and unit price, where relevant
- Sale amount and currency
- Discount and applicable tax
- Payment terms or status

For a small shop, a daily sales register can include both immediately paid and unpaid transactions, provided their payment status is clear. See [How to Record Daily Sales for a Small Business](/blog/how-to-record-daily-sales-small-business).

## 2. What does a cash book record?

A cash book records movements in the business's cash and bank accounts. It can include receipts from customers, payments to suppliers, bank charges, owner contributions, and other transactions that affect cash or bank balances.

A simple cash book entry may include:
- Transaction date
- Receipt or payment reference
- Description
- Account or category
- Money received
- Money paid
- Running balance
- Supporting document

A cash book should reflect actual receipts and payments, not simply the total value of invoices issued during the day.

For example, a business may issue an invoice for $200 today and receive $80 today. The sales record shows the sale, while the cash book or payment record shows the $80 receipt. The remaining $120 is still outstanding, subject to the transaction terms. This is an illustrative example, not a real transaction or universal accounting treatment.

## 3. What does a journal record?

In double-entry accounting, a journal records transactions using debit and credit entries. A business may use separate journals for frequent transaction types, such as sales, purchases, cash receipts, and cash payments.

The journal entry depends on the transaction and accounting method. For a sale on credit, the accounting entry often recognizes the receivable and revenue, with any applicable tax and cost-of-sales entries handled according to the business's system and applicable rules.

When the customer later pays, that receipt is recorded as a separate event. It reduces the receivable and increases cash or bank. It is not a second sale.

## 4. What does the general ledger do?

The general ledger groups journal entries into accounts such as cash, bank, accounts receivable, sales revenue, inventory, cost of goods sold, and operating expenses.

It helps answer questions such as:
- How much revenue has been recorded?
- What is the balance of accounts receivable?
- How much cash or bank balance is recorded?
- What inventory value is reflected in the accounts?
- What expenses have been recorded?

A ledger is not simply another daily sales list. It organizes accounting entries so that account balances and financial statements can be prepared.

## 5. Example: sale today, payment later

Imagine a business issues an invoice for $200 on Monday. The customer pays $80 on Monday and the remaining $120 on Friday. The example assumes one currency and excludes tax and fees for simplicity.

| Record                     | Monday                                                 | Friday                                          |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Sales record               | Record the $200 sale                                   | No new sale for this receipt                    |
| Payment record / cash book | Record $80 received                                    | Record $120 received                            |
| Customer balance           | $120 remains                                           | Balance becomes $0 after the full payment       |
| General ledger             | Record the sale and receipt under the accounting rules | Record the later receipt against the receivable |

The example demonstrates why sales and receipts should remain distinct. The Friday payment settles part of an earlier sale; it should not create a second $120 sale.

## 6. How the records connect

A simplified workflow is:

1. A sale occurs and supporting documentation is created.
2. The sale is entered in the sales records or sales journal.
3. A customer payment is entered when money is received.
4. The journal entries update the appropriate ledger accounts.
5. The business reconciles cash and bank records with available statements.
6. Account balances are used in financial reports.

In a small business, some of these steps may be automated or summarized in accounting software. The underlying distinction between a sale, a receipt, and a ledger account still matters.

## 7. Common mistakes to avoid

**Counting an invoice as money received.** An invoice records a sale or amount due; it does not prove that cash has arrived.

**Recording the later receipt as another sale.** Match it against the original invoice or customer balance.

**Mixing personal and business payments without explanation.** Keep business records clear and follow the process appropriate for the business structure.

**Adding amounts in different currencies.** Keep them separate or use documented exchange rates and accounting procedures.

**Failing to retain supporting documents.** Receipts, invoices, bank statements, and payment confirmations help explain entries in the books.

## 8. Which records does a small business need?

The answer depends on the type and size of the business, its accounting method, the volume of transactions, and local requirements. A small retailer may need a sales register, purchase records, payment records, stock records, and a general ledger. A service business may not need item-level inventory records but still needs sales, expense, customer, and payment information.

The IRS explains that journals and ledgers summarize supporting business documents, and that the recordkeeping system should suit the business and clearly show income and expenses. This is U.S. federal guidance, not a substitute for the rules in other countries. Read [How should I record my business transactions?](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/how-should-i-record-my-business-transactions).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a sales book the same as a cash book?

No. A sales book records sales. A cash book records receipts and payments that affect cash or bank accounts. A credit sale can be recorded before payment is received.

### Is a ledger the same as a journal?

No. A journal records transactions, typically with debit and credit entries. A ledger groups those entries by account and maintains account balances.

### Should a cash sale appear in both sales records and the cash book?

A cash sale may be summarized in the sales records and the cash receipt records. The accounting system should ensure that the transaction is posted correctly without recognizing the same revenue twice.

### Does a payment received create new revenue?

Not necessarily. A payment may settle an amount that was already recorded as a sale or receivable. The business should apply the accounting treatment that fits its system and circumstances.
